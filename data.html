01 — The problem we are built for

The schema is documented. The meaning is not.

The data dictionary says the column is called customer_status. It does not say that 4 means "dormant since 2018", that 4 was previously X until the migration in 2021, that the field is now ignored by the new platform but still populated by the legacy ETL job that runs nightly.

Every modern data team inherits this. The schema survives. The intent doesn't. The analyst who built the pipeline left. The regulator changes the reporting standard. A migration silently changes how a field is populated and nobody flags it. The compliance report still runs. The numbers still come out. Nobody knows whether they still mean what they used to mean.

This is the same problem CodeDoctor solves for code. The institutional knowledge of what the data is supposed to represent was never in any system. It was in the people who built the pipeline. We extract it, validate it, encode it.

02 — How we approach it

Field-level intent, recovered from the people who still know.

Extract structure from the archive. Surface drift across migrations. Validate field-level meaning with the humans who built the pipeline. Encode the result as a queryable rulebook.

  • i.
    Archive ingestion.

    The data warehouse, the ETL job history, the schema migrations, the lineage logs. Everything that describes where data came from and how it has changed. The first deliverable is a coherent picture of how the warehouse actually got to its current shape.

  • ii.
    Field-level pattern surfacing.

    Cardinality shifts that don't match documented changes. Fields whose distribution changed silently after a migration. Compliance fields whose population pattern doesn't match the regulatory definition. LLMs read across the schema and the data simultaneously.

  • iii.
    Pipeline-author validation.

    For every surfaced pattern, a structured prompt to the data engineer, analyst, or business operator who knows what the field is supposed to represent. The institutional answer — "that flag was added in 2019 for the FCA Stage-2 reporting; we stopped using it but the ETL kept populating it" — becomes a structured constraint.

  • iv.
    Field rulebook.

    A structured, machine-readable representation of every field's intended meaning, its valid populations, its compliance lineage, and the constraints that should hold on it. The warehouse becomes queryable — by your AI tools, by your compliance team, by the regulator — against the meaning that actually applies.

03 — Where it fits

For data functions where the question is no longer can we query it — it is can we trust what comes back.

  • 01.
    Regulated reporting.

    FCA, PRA, EBA, DORA, NYDFS — every regime where the reporting definition has changed faster than the warehouse has. Where compliance reports run, but nobody is certain the underlying fields still mean what they used to.

  • 02.
    Post-migration warehouses.

    The warehouse survived the migration. Did the field-level meaning? Nobody knows, and the new data team can't reconstruct it from documentation that doesn't exist.

  • 03.
    AI-readiness.

    Before your analytics copilot can give the business honest answers, the warehouse needs a meaning layer the copilot can read against. Generic LLMs cannot infer what your fields are supposed to represent. Ours doesn't try — it asks the humans who know.
